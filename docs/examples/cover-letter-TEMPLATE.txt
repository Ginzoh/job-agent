How to write your cover-letter file
===================================

Copy this into `cover_letter_example/` as a .txt or .md file and rewrite it in your own voice.

This file is NOT used as a form to fill in. It is read as a sample of how you write, so the
generator can produce letters that sound like you rather than like a template. That means the
most useful thing you can put here is one real letter you were happy with — your actual
sentences, your actual rhythm, your actual level of formality.

What makes the difference:

  - Write it the way you would actually write to a person: the vocabulary and formality of
    this file are what get reproduced.
  - Open plainly. Say which role it is and one concrete thing about yourself. Resist the urge
    to start with a scene, an aphorism or a general truth about the industry ("There is a
    moment in every project when...") — it reads as warming up, and whatever opening device
    you use here, the generator will imitate it on every letter it writes.
  - Include at least one specific story with real detail. Specificity is the thing the
    generator learns most from, and generic source material produces generic output.
  - Leave your placeholders in square brackets — [Company], [Role title], [Date]. They are
    recognised as slots to fill rather than text to copy.
  - Length matters less than voice. A letter you would genuinely send beats a polished one you
    would not.

Below is a neutral skeleton to replace. Do not send this as-is; it exists to show the shape.

---


[Your Name]
[Your title] — [Your company, if you have one]
[City, Country]
[email] · [phone] · [LinkedIn] · [GitHub / Portfolio]


[Date]


To: [Hiring Manager / Recruiter name]
[Company name]


Subject: Application — [Role title]


Dear [Name],


Say which role this is about and the single most relevant thing about you — no preamble. Then
one concrete piece of evidence: something you built, and what made it non-trivial. The reader
should know why you are worth reading by the third line.


Second paragraph: your stack, and why it is your stack. What you can follow from end to end.
Anchor it in something you actually shipped rather than listing technologies.


Third paragraph: one role or project in real detail. What you owned, what constraint made it
hard, and what you learned that you would bring here. This is where specificity earns you the
interview.


Fourth paragraph: the thing a CV compresses badly. A side project, a technology you picked up
unprompted, a way of working you care about. Ideally something that shows you learn without
being told to.


If there is an obvious objection — fewer years than they asked for, a gap, a stack they use
that you do not — address it briefly and without apology. A screener who spots it themselves
weighs it more heavily than one you have already answered.


Close with what you are looking for, what you offer, and your availability. Keep it short.


Kind regards,


[Your Name]
[Company / SIRET if freelance]
[email] · [phone]
